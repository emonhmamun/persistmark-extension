The Art of Deep Reading in a Distracted World

By Amara Chen  ·  October 2026  ·  9 min read

Reading deeply is becoming a rare skill. The average reader now skims hundreds of fragments a day — headlines, notifications, captions — while spending barely a few uninterrupted minutes with any single text. Yet the research is unambiguous: comprehension, empathy and long-term memory all depend on sustained, focused reading.

The good news is that deep reading is a habit, not a talent. It responds remarkably well to small environmental changes: a printed book instead of a screen, a timer, a quiet room, and most importantly — an active conversation with the text.

Marking is thinking

When you underline or highlight a passage, you are forced to make a decision: this matters, that does not. That tiny act of judgment is where understanding begins. Studies of academic readers show that selective marking improves recall far more than re-reading, because every mark is a small commitment — a signature of attention.

“The marks we leave on a page are the footprints of our attention.”

Annotations compound over time. A book you have marked becomes a map of your past self — you can return to a chapter years later and meet the person you were when you first read it, argument by argument, line by line.

Build a reading loop

Deep readers follow a loop: read, mark, revisit. The final step is the one most of us skip. A weekly review of your highlights — ten minutes on a Sunday evening — turns scattered notes into durable knowledge. The highlights you return to become quotes you can use; the ones you never revisit quietly tell you what no longer matters to you.

Start small. One article a day, read without interruption, three passages marked and one sentence rewritten in your own words. Within a month the habit will feel less like discipline and more like appetite.
